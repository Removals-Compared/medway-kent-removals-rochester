How to Move House with Elderly Parents: A Calm Plan

By Amos Osho, Founder of Medway and Kent Removals | November 2026

The kindest way to move house with an elderly parent is to let them lead the decisions, start earlier than feels necessary, and plan moving day around their routine, medication and energy rather than around the van. The boxes are the easy part. What decides whether the move goes well is who is making the choices, how belongings are sorted, and whether the first night in the new home feels settled. This guide takes it in that order.

Start with the conversation, not the boxes

Before anyone mentions packing, have an unhurried conversation. Choose a quiet moment, not a stressful one such as straight after a fall or a hospital visit if you can help it. Ask open questions: what do you want from your next home, what worries you about moving, what would you hate to lose? Listen first. A parent who feels they are being managed will dig in, and a parent who feels heard often starts the practical planning themselves.

Where they are able, the parent should make the decisions. Your role is to supply information, do the legwork and point out risks, not to decide for them. Ask them what matters most and write it down. Common answers include:

  • Staying near family, friends, a church or a favourite shop
  • Having a garden, even a small one
  • Avoiding stairs
  • Keeping a spare room for visitors
  • Being close to their GP and pharmacy

Next, work out who needs to be involved. Siblings should hear the plan early so that nobody is surprised later. If a carer visits, tell them the dates. If there is a power of attorney in place, or the parent is thinking about one, or the move involves selling a home, it is sensible to speak to a solicitor. We can only offer general pointers here, not legal advice. Likewise, for sheltered housing or care home questions, the local council and the parent's GP are the right places to start.

Finally, agree a rough timetable and choose one person as the single point of contact for the move. That person talks to the removal company, the estate agent or landlord, and the family. It stops three people giving three different answers.

Choosing a home that works for their mobility

Think about the next ten years, not just the next one. When you view a property with your parent, check the practical details as well as the feel of the place:

  • Stairs and steps. Is there step-free access to the front door? Are bedroom and bathroom on the same level?
  • Bathroom layout. Is there a walk-in shower or room to adapt one? Is the toilet easy to reach at night?
  • Distance to everyday services. Shops, GP surgery, pharmacy and a bus stop within a realistic walk.
  • Parking and access. Can a large van get close on moving day? Can family park nearby for regular visits afterwards?

Measure before you decide what comes along. Take the dimensions of the new living room and bedroom and compare them with the parent's key pieces: the favourite chair, the bed, the sideboard, the table. It is far easier to say no to a wardrobe on paper than on the doorstep. Sketching a simple floor plan with your parent can make this an enjoyable exercise rather than a loss.

If your parent is moving in with you, prepare their room before they arrive. Think about a bed at a comfortable height, good lighting including a lamp within reach of the bed, clear floor space, and somewhere to sit quietly. A space that is clearly theirs, with their own furniture and photographs, matters more than most people expect.

Sorting and downsizing with kindness

Sorting is where most family rows happen, so give it time. Start early and work in short sessions of an hour or so, one room or even one drawer at a time. Stop when your parent tires. Begin with rooms that carry less emotion, such as the spare room, the loft or the garage, and leave the kitchen table photographs and the bedroom until later when you have built some momentum.

A simple four-pile method keeps things moving:

  1. Keep. Comes to the new home.
  2. Family. Goes to a named relative, now rather than later.
  3. Donate or sell. Leaves the house, but with the parent's agreement.
  4. Dispose. Genuinely finished with: broken, expired or unusable.

For sentimental items, avoid pressure and avoid the phrase "you don't need this". Instead, offer ways to keep the memory without the object. Photograph a collection of ornaments, a child's school work or a wardrobe of clothes. Set up one memory box for the things that cannot be replaced by a photograph. Passing items to relatives while your parent is still here often helps, because they get to see the object go to someone who will value it and hear the story attached.

Some things should never go on the van. Keep paperwork, medication, jewellery, cash, passports, keys and any valuables with your parent or with the trusted helper on the day. Put them in one bag and keep it in a known place. For more detail on getting through the volume of belongings, see our guide on how to declutter before a house move.

A week-by-week timeline

Every family is different, but this is a realistic shape for a move that does not feel rushed.

Four to six weeks before

  • Have the decision conversation and agree the timetable
  • Choose your single point of contact
  • Get quotes from removal companies and compare what each includes
  • Tell key contacts that a move is coming, including carers and close family

Two to three weeks before

  • Sort belongings using the four piles
  • Pack non-essentials such as books, spare linen, out-of-season clothes and ornaments
  • Label every box by the room it will go to in the new home, not the room it came from

The week before

  • Pack the essentials box (see below)
  • Confirm access and parking at both addresses
  • Arrange the helper whose only job on the day is your parent
  • Check the new home has working heating, a made bed and a clear path from the door

Packing: how much to do yourselves and what to hand over

Family can sensibly take on soft, low-risk jobs such as clothes, books, linen and general kitchen items. Packing alongside your parent can also be a good way to talk about what each thing means. Hand over the awkward and fragile jobs: glassware, crockery, framed pictures and anything that would be heartbreaking to break. Lifting and bending for long periods is also tiring and risky for an older person, so keep them to light tasks and sitting down jobs.

Our packing service covers full or partial packing with materials, priced per job. Many families choose partial packing, doing the easy rooms themselves and leaving the kitchen and breakables to us. Ask for a quote on the exact split you want.

Whatever you pack, make up a clearly labelled box of daily essentials that travels with your parent, not on the van:

  • Medication and any prescriptions
  • Glasses, hearing aid batteries and chargers
  • Kettle, favourite mug, tea, coffee and a few simple foods
  • Bedding, towels and night clothes for the first night
  • Phone numbers written on paper, including the GP and family
  • A few familiar photographs or ornaments

Moving day, planned around the parent

Loading is noisy, busy and full of strangers carrying familiar things out of the door. Where possible, your parent should not be in the house for it. Ask a trusted person to take them somewhere calm, perhaps a relative's home or a quiet café, and make that person's only job looking after your parent. They should not be answering the crew's questions or checking boxes.

Build the day around the parent's body clock:

  • Allow for proper rest, not just a quick sit down
  • Plan meals and drinks at normal times
  • Know where toilets are at every stop
  • Write down medication times and set alarms
  • Keep walking to a minimum and have a chair available at both ends

If any part of the old home needs a quiet room to stay in while the crew works, keep one room clear until last, with a chair, a drink and the essentials box.

At the new home, ask the crew to set up the bed first, then a chair, a lamp and the kettle, so that the first evening feels like home even if boxes are still stacked. Your point of contact should walk through the new home with the crew so each item is placed in the correct room, using the labels. Tell them early if something needs to go in a particular spot, for example a chair by a window or a table that must be kept clear of walkways.

It is worth being clear about what we do. The crew moves and places furniture and belongings. We are a removals company, not a care service, so we cannot provide personal care, help with medication or look after your parent during the day. That is why the dedicated helper matters.

Settling in after the move

In the first week, keep routines as familiar as possible: same meal times, same morning cup of tea, same television programme. Put the furniture in a similar arrangement to the old home where space allows, and get photographs on the walls early, because a wall of familiar faces does a great deal of work.

Over the following days, deal with the admin. Update the GP, pharmacy, bank, pension provider and utilities. Our guide to the full list of who to tell about a new address is a useful checklist, and it is worth doing this on your parent's behalf with their agreement.

Help your parent meet the neighbours and find the local shop, library or café, and put regular visits in the diary so that they do not drop away once the excitement of moving has passed. Expect tiredness. Many people also feel low or unsettled for a while after leaving a long-term home, and that is understandable. Give it time, but if the low mood continues, or you are worried about their health, suggest a chat with their GP.

When storage or a phased move helps

A single big day is not always right. If the old home sells before the new one is ready, or if your parent is moving to a much smaller space, short and long term storage can bridge the gap or hold the furniture you are not ready to part with. Frequently asked questions

How do I talk to my elderly parent about moving house?

Pick a calm moment, ask open questions about what matters to them, and listen before offering solutions. Let them lead the decisions where they can, and avoid presenting the move as already settled.

What should an elderly person keep with them on moving day?

Medication, glasses, chargers, phone, keys, important papers, valuables, a change of clothes and a few comforting essentials such as a favourite mug. Keep them in one bag, not on the van.

How far in advance should we plan a move for an elderly parent?

Four to six weeks is a sensible minimum for the decisions, quotes and sorting. More time is better if there is a lot to go through or if your parent tires easily. Same-day moves are possible subject to availability, but a planned move is calmer.

Can removal men help with packing for an elderly person?

Yes. Our packing service covers full or partial packing with materials, priced per job. Many families do clothes and books themselves and hand over fragile items. The crew does not provide personal care.

What can we do with belongings an elderly parent does not have room for?

Pass them to relatives now, photograph them for a memory box, sell or donate them, or keep them in storage while your parent decides. Storage is priced per week by volume.
