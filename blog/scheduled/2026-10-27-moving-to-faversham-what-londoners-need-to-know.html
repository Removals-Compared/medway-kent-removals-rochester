Faversham suits Londoners who want more space and a calmer pace, and who are happy to trade some of London's convenience for it. Why Londoners look at Faversham

The reasons people give for leaving London are usually familiar. They want more space for the same budget, a garden or a quieter street, and a pace of life that feels less crowded. Some are priced out of buying. Some are renting and tired of short tenancies. Others have had a child, or started working from home more often, and the flat no longer fits.

Faversham will not be right for everyone. If you rely on late-night variety on your doorstep, or your work needs you in central London five days a week, a smaller town may feel like a compromise. If you are happy to plan your evenings a little more and value green space and a town centre you can walk across, it may suit you well. The rest of this guide is meant to help you judge that for yourself.

What Faversham is like to live in

In general terms, Faversham is a market town with a historic centre, a creek and surrounding countryside. That mix is what draws many people from London: you get a proper town with shops and services, with open land close by. We have deliberately not listed specific shops, markets, festivals or pubs here, because those change and you should see them for yourself.

The best advice we can give is to visit twice before you commit. Go on a weekday and see how the town feels at school drop-off, at lunchtime and in the early evening. Then go on a weekend and see how busy the centre gets, where people park and how easy it is to get around on foot. A town can feel very different on a quiet Tuesday and a busy Saturday, and you want to know which version you are signing up for.

Also look beyond the town itself. Many people who say they are moving to Faversham end up in a surrounding village or on a rural road. That can be lovely, but it changes the practical picture. Country lanes can be narrow, driveways can be long or unmade, and a house may sit well away from a main road. None of this is a problem if your removal company knows about it in advance. It becomes a problem when it is a surprise on the day.

The commute and the trade-offs

If you still need to get to London for work, the commute is the single biggest thing to test. Do not rely on a blog, including this one, for journey times or fares. Check current train times, ticket prices and season ticket options directly with the train operator, and look at how they apply to the days you would actually travel.

Then think through the practical questions that the timetable does not answer:

  • Station parking. If you plan to drive to the station, find out how much parking there is, what it costs and whether you can realistically get a space at your usual time.
  • Working from home days. A hybrid pattern of two or three office days is a very different proposition from five. It changes whether a season ticket or pay-as-you-go makes more sense.
  • The school run. If you have children, work out how the school journey fits around your train. A commute that works on paper can fall apart when you add drop-off times.
  • Late returns. Check the last convenient trains home if your work or social life in London runs late.

The most useful thing you can do is a trial commute at the real hours you would travel. Leave at the time you would leave, take the train you would take, and do the return journey at the end of a working day. One honest test is worth more than any figure we could quote you.

London access problems to sort before moving day

Access is where London moves most often differ from moves in a Kent town. A van that is straightforward to load in a quiet road can be much harder on a busy London street, and the same applies in reverse at the other end. The fixed quote is held provided the property contents and access are as described, so the more accurately you describe both, the more reliable the quote.

At the London end, tell us about anything that affects loading:

  • Lifts and stairs. Is there a lift, and is it large enough for furniture? How many flights of stairs are there if not? Are there tight turns or narrow stairwells?
  • Narrow streets. Can a Luton van get close to the door, or will it need to park some distance away?
  • Parking permits and suspensions. Many London streets are permit controlled. If you need a bay suspended or a permit arranged, speak to your local council in good time, and tell us what you have arranged.
  • Shared entrances. Managed blocks sometimes have rules on move times or lift bookings. Check with your landlord or managing agent.

At the Faversham end, think about the same questions. Where will the van park outside your new home? Is the road narrow, or does it have parked cars on both sides? If you are moving to a village or rural lane, is there a tight bend, a low branch, a long driveway or a gate? Tell us what the crew should expect, and mention anything the seller or landlord has said about access. A photo of the front of the property, or the street, can help.

Planning timeline and what to book first

A move from London to Kent has a sensible order. Roughly:

  1. View and decide. Visit Faversham, test the commute and settle on a property or a shortlist.
  2. Get a fixed quote. Describe your contents and the access at both ends as accurately as you can.
  3. Book and pay the deposit. This secures your date. Remember the quote is valid for 14 days.
  4. Declutter. Moving less costs less and makes the day easier. Sort what to keep, sell, donate or dispose of.
  5. Pack. Start with things you rarely use and leave everyday items to last.
  6. Confirm access. Check parking, permits, lift bookings and any key arrangements at both ends.
  7. Move. Keep documents, keys, chargers and essentials with you.

Checklist before you commit to Faversham

Before you sign anything, work through this list:

  • Visit on a weekday and a weekend, and at different times of day.
  • Do a trial commute at the hours you would really travel.
  • Check broadband availability for the specific address with providers directly.
  • Check parking at the property, at the station and near the places you will visit regularly.
  • Look at schools and GP registration directly with the schools, the local authority and the practices. Do not rely on second-hand summaries.
  • Walk the roads around the property and note any narrow lanes or access issues to tell your removal company.
  • Compare two or three removal quotes on a like-for-like basis, including what is and is not included.
